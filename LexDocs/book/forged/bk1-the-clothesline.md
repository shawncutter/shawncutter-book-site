# The Clothesline

Kristin was on the back of the three-wheeler, arms around me, the way she always was. That's how it worked on the farm — if there was a machine and it was moving, there was a Cutter kid on it, and usually a second one hanging off the back. We didn't think about it. Nobody thought about it. Helmets on, gas in the tank, go.

We rode under a chain-link fence. I don't even remember why the fence was strung the way it was — half the things on a farm are rigged up temporary and then live for twenty years — but there was a length of chain hanging down at head height, and as we went under it, I did the natural thing. I threw it up and over my helmet to get it past me.

And it dropped. Right down into the gap between my helmet and hers.

I hit the gas.

I found out later exactly what a clothesline is. The chain caught her across the top of the chest and the throat and it ripped her clean off the back of the machine. One second she's holding onto me, the next she's gone, and she's on the ground behind me.

Here's the part I've had to make my peace with over the years: we were basically stopped. Barely rolling. In the actual physics of it, it wasn't much. But in the moment — in my body — it was the worst thing I had ever done. I turned around and my little sister was on the ground and the only thought I had was *I could have ripped her head off.* That's not a figure of speech I reach for lightly. That's the exact sentence that went through my head. Thank God she had the helmet on. Thank God for the one piece of safety gear we bothered with.

She was fine. Scared, rattled, but fine. And that's the thing about the farm — the margin between a story you laugh about and a story nobody ever tells again was about six inches and one helmet strap. We were always living in that margin and we never once acknowledged it.

Gia and I were up in Ohio a few weeks ago, and I told her this story. She's the age now where she needs to hear what a machine can actually do to you, and I've learned you can't lecture a kid into it — you have to let them see the near-miss. So I told her about the chain and the gap and the gas. And Kristin was right there, and we're both laughing about it now, because we can. Because nothing happened. Because the universe let us keep that one.

That's how these stories work in our family. They start as terror and they age into lessons. The clothesline isn't a story about how reckless we were, even though we were. It's the story I now use to explain to my own kids why you respect the machine — because I have a specific, personal, throat-level memory of what it costs when you don't.
