# Operator in Command

My father was sixteen years old the night the road took something from him that it never gave back.

He was on a motorcycle outside Albuquerque, Ohio — going fast, the way sixteen goes fast, with a friend riding on the back and the dark pressing in on both sides. Somewhere ahead, a farmer had pulled a tractor to the shoulder and walked away from it. No lights. No signs. No reason for two boys at speed to know it was there until it already was.

Chuck hit the back of it.

I've turned that half-second over in my head more times than I can count, because of what happened inside it. At the last instant the boy on the back saw the tractor coming and did the only thing a body does when it wants to live — he shoved himself forward, away from the impact, into my father's back. And that shove pinned Chuck to the machine. He couldn't fly. The handlebars caught his legs and crushed them, and he stayed bolted to that motorcycle through all of it, an operator who could not leave the controls.

Three months in a hospital bed put him back together, more or less. The "more or less" lived in his right knee for the rest of his life — a knot of scar tissue so thick the joint was visibly bigger than the other one. You could see it across a room. Decades later it was the blockage in that same leg that needed stents, and the medication for those stents, and the thin blood and the dismissed warnings that were all sitting at the table the morning his heart stopped at fifty-nine.

So the accident never actually ended. It just went quiet and waited. It followed him from sixteen all the way to the last morning, the way a debt follows you — patient, compounding, due eventually.

I know something about that.

I have my own version, lower down, in my foot. My own half-second that rewired the whole arithmetic of my life — forty surgeries, the scooter, the placard, the scar tissue I carry the way he carried his. And here's the part I couldn't have written when I was younger, the part that gave me chills the day I finally said it out loud:

We were both the operator in command.

That's the phrase. Not victim. Not bystander. Not unlucky kid in the wrong place. Operator. He was driving that motorcycle. I was running my own machine the day mine happened. Sixteen and ten — different ages, different vehicles, the same fundamental truth that the man at the controls owns the outcome even when the road is the one that cheated. It was an accident *and* it was accountability. Both things, in the same breath, with no contradiction between them. The tractor had no lights. He was still the one driving.

I think that's the inheritance I actually got from my father — more than the oilfield language, more than the work ethic, more than the intensity that made cold calls land because I already spoke the dialect. What I got from Chuck was the knowledge, carried in the body before it was ever spoken, that a man can be wronged by the world and still be responsible for his own machine. That the two are not enemies. That you take the hit, you sit in the controls you can't abandon, and you spend the next forty years deciding what to do with the knee — or the foot — that's never going to be the same.

He never told me any of this. He probably never framed it this way himself. But it was the operating system underneath everything he was, and it got installed in me long before I understood what it was.

Sixteen and ten. Two boys, two accidents, two scars that outlived the day they were made.

Both of us, the operator in command.
