# Riding Shotgun

Before I knew it was an education, I thought it was just a ride.

The passenger seat of my father's truck was where I spent the hours that built me. I didn't know that's what was happening. I thought I was killing time, watching the fields go by, listening to a man talk on the phone. But that truck was a classroom, and Chuck was teaching the whole time without ever once calling it a lesson.

Here is the thing about my father that took me decades to understand: he was a farmer. That was the root of him, under everything else. He came from ground that his own people had farmed and cared for — firmly rooted points in space and time, a particular patch of earth that tied the Cutters to the land the way it ties me to it still. And then he did something I find quietly remarkable. He figured out how to reach *under* that ground and pull energy out of it. He learned to drill. He learned to produce oil and gas. He took the farmer's relationship with the surface of the land and extended it straight down into the rock.

That's the part people miss when they hear "oilfield." Chuck wasn't an extractor who showed up, took what he wanted, and left a mess. He worked with farmers and landowners because he *was* one. He treated their land the way he treated his own farm — like something you're accountable to, something you return to, something with a name and a memory. And that's how he built trust. Not with a pitch. With the simple, legible fact that he understood what their dirt meant to them because he knew exactly what his meant to him.

I inherited all of that from the passenger seat.

I inherited it riding around in that truck, hearing him work the phone — and the man could *work* a phone. He made deals. He hit obstacles and went through them, around them, under them, whatever the obstacle required. "Make shit happen" wasn't a slogan he taught me; it was just the ambient weather of being near him. Things got done because he decided they would and then refused to be told otherwise. I absorbed the cadence of it before I could have explained a single transaction. The directness. The trust-building. The way you talk to a landowner like a neighbor because he is one.

That phone, by the way, was not free.

The phone bill came into the Cutter house like a small storm — all those deals worked from the cab of a truck added up, and it landed on the kitchen table as tension, one of many flash points in a household that had no shortage of them. I file that away honestly, because the inheritance wasn't clean. The same drive that built the trust and made the deals also rang up the bill and lit the fuse at home. You don't get the one without the other. The engine that powers a man powers all of him, the good rooms and the hard ones alike.

So now look at where I ended up.

Energy. Land. A farm. Trust built by showing people I understand what their ground means because I know what mine means. I didn't set out to recreate my father's life — I'd have told you I was building something brand new, something he wouldn't even fully recognize. And he wouldn't recognize all of it. But he'd recognize the bones. A Cutter, rooted to a particular piece of earth, figuring out how to draw energy from it, and earning the right to do it by treating the land and the people on it like they matter.

We are both farmers. We were always both farmers. He just rode in the truck a generation earlier, and I was the kid in the passenger seat, taking notes I didn't know I was taking.
