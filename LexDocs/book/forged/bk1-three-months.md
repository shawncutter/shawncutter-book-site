# Three Months

## I looked at that knee my entire childhood and never once asked about it.

It was the wrong size. That's the first thing. His right knee was visibly bigger than his left — not swollen, not temporary, just permanently larger, the way a fence post gets larger where the wire cut into it and the tree grew around the wound. Scar tissue that had been there so long it had stopped being an injury and become part of the architecture of the man.

You'd see it in the summer. Shorts, the lake, working outside. It was just there, the way his mustache was there.

I knew the summary the way you know everything in a house like that — as inherited fact, delivered once and never expanded. Chuck had been on a motorcycle. Somebody left a tractor in the road. He hit it. Three months in a hospital.

Four sentences. That was the entire estate.

---

Here's what the four sentences leave out.

He was sixteen. It was night. He was going very fast, which is not a detail anyone had to add for me, because I never once heard a story about my father on a motorcycle where he was going a reasonable speed.

There was a friend on the back.

And the tractor wasn't in the middle of the road. It was parked on the side of it — sitting there with no lights, no reflectors, no signs, no flares, none of the things that would be legally required now and were apparently optional in rural Ohio in 1972. Someone had finished a job and walked away from a two-ton machine on the shoulder of a dark road.

He hit the back of it.

---

The part I didn't have until this year is the part that changes the whole story.

At the last second — and I mean the last second, the interval where there is no decision, only reflex — the friend on the back saw the tractor.

And what he did was throw himself forward into Chuck's back.

He pinned him. He drove his own body weight into my father and held him against the bike so that when it hit, Chuck couldn't be thrown.

I've turned that over a lot. Every instinct in a human body at that moment says *get away from the impact.* His said *hold him.* And I don't know if it was calculation or pure animal reflex or something in between, and I'll never know, because nobody in my family remembers his name.

The handlebars crushed my father's legs. That's the price of not being thrown — you stay with the machine, and the machine folds around you.

But he stayed with the machine. And a man who gets thrown off a motorcycle into a dark road at speed in 1972 does not necessarily become anybody's father.

Somewhere out there, or more likely dead by now, is a man I have never met, whose name I don't have, who is the reason I exist. He gets four words in the family record. *There was a passenger.*

---

Three months in a hospital.

I have no idea what those three months were. Not one detail. My father, who would tell you at length about the time he tied a full-grown Holstein steer in a friend's front yard in the city of Wooster, never told me a single thing about ninety days on his back at sixteen with his legs in pieces.

I know what ninety days does, because I did my own version of it at ten. I know what the ceiling looks like. I know what the nights are.

He knew that I knew. I knew that he knew. And we never said one word about it to each other, which is its own whole piece and I've already written it.

What he did with his ninety days was decide that the answer to being broken by a motorcycle was to go faster, and to put his kids on machines before they could ride bicycles. I've written that one too.

This piece is about what the ninety days did to him on a longer timescale.

---

The knee never stopped being the injured knee.

That's the thing people get wrong about a body that's been rebuilt. There's no point where it converts back to standard. It's a repair, and a repair is a different object than the original, and it ages differently. Mine does. His did.

Decades later, that same leg developed a blockage. Circulation in a limb that had been crushed, rebuilt, and carried around on scar tissue for thirty-plus years. They went in and put in stents.

After the stent surgery he was on blood thinners.

And it was the combination — the thinners plus another medication — that killed him. November 12th, 2010. Around 5:15 in the morning. Fifty-four years old.

CJ called me at 3:30.

---

So here's the line, and I've checked it a dozen times looking for the place it breaks:

In 1972, somebody parked a tractor on the side of a dark road and didn't put a light on it.

In 2010, that killed my father.

Thirty-eight years of latency. The injury didn't end in the hospital at sixteen. It went subclinical. It sat inside the leg doing quiet damage on a schedule nobody was tracking, and then it presented as a blockage, and the treatment for the blockage is what took him out at fifty-four.

I want to be honest about the limits of what I'm claiming. I'm not a doctor. I can't tell you the crushed leg is medically airtight as the root cause of the blockage — bodies are messier than fault trees and Chuck Cutter did plenty of other things to his cardiovascular system over fifty-four years. A cardiologist would give you a more careful sentence than mine.

But I've spent my whole career tracing failures back through systems, and when you find a component that was catastrophically damaged, imperfectly repaired, never re-inspected, and then failed at exactly that point — you write it up. You don't get to ignore it because the timeline is inconvenient.

Thirty-eight years is a long latency. It is not an impossible one.

---

What do I do with it?

I've tried it three ways.

**Fate** doesn't work for me. I lost my access to that at ten years old in a hospital bed telling a pastor there was no plan. I'm not going to pick it back up now because it would make my father's death rhyme nicely.

**Bad luck** is true and useless. Everything is bad luck if you back up far enough.

What's left is **physics**, and physics turns out to be the most unsettling of the three. Because physics says there was no author. Nobody chose this. A man finished a job in 1972 and was tired and didn't set out a light, and that ordinary, forgivable, five-second omission propagated forward through a sixteen-year-old's legs, through three months, through a knee that was always the wrong size, through thirty-eight years, through a stent, through a prescription — and arrived at 5:15 on a November morning.

Nobody was aiming. That's the part I can't get comfortable with. I could hold a grudge against a person. You can't hold a grudge against a chain.

And I know exactly why this one lands on me the way it does. Because I ran the same analysis on my own accident when I was ten — the bolt, the twenty minutes, the empty gas tank, the dropped wagon, the two seconds. I built the fault tree for my own life before I was eleven years old.

I just never thought to run it on his until this year.

Same kind of road. Same kind of dark. Same casual absence of a light where a light should have been. Two Cutters, thirty-eight years and one generation apart, both taken apart by somebody else's ordinary Tuesday.

He got ninety days and a bad knee, and it caught up with him at fifty-four.
I got forty surgeries and a foot made out of my back, and I'm forty-six and still walking on it.

I don't know what to call that except the family business.

**Cutter Rule:** What doesn't kill you sets a timer. Find out how long it's been running.
