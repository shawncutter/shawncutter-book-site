# The Black Snake

There was a big black snake crossing the driveway down at the end, right by where we waited for the bus. Big one. And I did what any farm kid does when he sees a snake, which is not leave it alone. I grabbed it.

Bad grab. The thing immediately went for me — whipping its head back around, trying to bite, all muscle and bad attitude. So I did the second thing a farm kid does, which is panic slightly and then commit fully. I slammed it against a tree.

It wrapped. Whipped itself around the trunk a couple times from the force of it. I let go. And then — because apparently once wasn't enough — I grabbed it again and swung even harder.

That second swing is the one that lives in the family. I put everything into it, and when it hit, the snake's guts came out of its mouth. Straight out. And they went all over Kristin.

All over her. Right there at the end of the driveway.

Right before the bus came.

I wish I could tell you there was some cleanup, some grace, some moment where the older brother made it right. There wasn't. The bus was coming. You went to school wearing whatever the morning gave you.

I tell this one now and it's pure comedy — the timing of it, the school bus, the sheer *of course it did* of the guts going exactly the wrong direction. But underneath the comedy there's the actual texture of what it was to grow up out there. Nobody was watching. Nobody was managing our risk or our exposure to the grosser facts of life and death. A snake was crossing the driveway and two kids dealt with it, badly, and then got on a bus and went to fourth grade.

Kristin and I have a whole catalog like this. Most of them I was the cause of and she was the collateral. And somehow that's the glue — the clothesline, the snake, all of it. You don't stay close to somebody for fifty years because everything went smoothly. You stay close because you both remember the driveway, and the snake, and the exact look on her face, and you're the only two people on earth who were there for it.
