# Disney Every Month

## I can't feel it the way she does. So I show up instead.

**Excerpt (Medium):** I learned how to be a father by watching my wife be a mother. I never had a full-time example of how parents are supposed to be. And after forty years of armor, I still don't feel for my kids what she feels for hers. So I stopped waiting to feel it. I started showing up instead.

Once a month, I take my daughters to Disney. They're fourteen and sixteen now, which means the window is closing — sixteen doesn't want to hold your hand, and fourteen is learning not to. I know what's coming. In a few years the trips stop, not because I cancel them but because the seats next to me fill up with other people's plans. So I'm buying the time while it's still for sale.

I can afford it now. That's not a small thing to say out loud, and I'm going to say it plainly, because the money is part of the story. For most of my life there wasn't enough margin to choose presence over the next thing. There was always a next thing — a well, a deal, a company, a fire. A few years ago I did the math that has nothing to do with money and realized it would be a tragedy to have built all of this and not enjoy the only part that doesn't sell: a Tuesday with my kids that I'll never get back.

So I made it a standing appointment. Every month. No exceptions I can help.

Here's the part I don't get to dress up.

I learned how to be a good father by watching Carina be a good mother. I didn't have the template. The house I grew up in had a lot of things — work, intensity, a father who was loud and getting softer too late — but it didn't have a full-time, steady example of what a parent is supposed to be. Most kids absorb that without knowing they're absorbing it. I had to study it like a foreign language, in my forties, by watching the woman I married do it on instinct.

She looks at our daughters and something moves in her that I can see from across the room. I have spent a long time trying to locate the same thing in myself, and I want to be honest about what I find, because a book full of redemption with no receipts isn't worth writing.

I don't feel it the way she does.

When Gia was born — thirty-two hours of labor, the baby in distress, the whole room holding its breath — I held her and waited for the wave everyone tells you about. The one that's supposed to rearrange you. It didn't come. I felt the importance of the moment the way you feel the importance of a number on a page. I knew it mattered. I couldn't feel that it mattered. I spent so many years in the rational part of my head that the emotional part learned to stay quiet, and it doesn't come back just because you finally want it to.

I know where the wall came from. A road in Ohio, a car I didn't see, forty surgeries, a kid who learned to turn the feeling off because the feeling was a fire he couldn't survive feeling all the way. The armor saved me. It also charged me, and it's still collecting — three decades later, in a maternity ward, in a Disney line, in the specific ache of loving people you can't feel as loudly as they deserve.

For a long time I thought the work was to tear the wall down. Feel it correctly. Catch up to Carina. I went to therapy. I did the reps. And some of it moved — I feel more now than I did at thirty, more than I did the day Gia was born. But I've stopped believing the wall comes all the way down, and I've stopped letting that be the thing that decides whether I'm a good father.

Because here is what I figured out, and it's the only useful thing I have to offer anyone built like me:

You don't have to feel it to show up for it.

Carina shows up because she feels it. I show up *instead* of feeling it all the way, and the showing up turns out to be the part the kids actually keep. They don't have access to what's happening behind my eyes. They have access to the fact that Dad is here, again, this month, like clockwork, on the teacups. The presence is real even when the feeling lags. Maybe especially then — because it cost me something to manufacture what comes free to other people, and I paid it anyway.

My father's last lesson, the one he left me before a heart attack took him at fifty-four, was this: work hard, play hard, spend time with family. Leave some work for tomorrow. Because if tomorrow never comes, the work doesn't matter. He learned it too late to use much of it. He got six months with Gia. I got a warning.

So I take the warning to Disney once a month.

I don't always feel what I'm supposed to feel standing in that park. But I am standing there. My daughters know where to find me. And if the feeling won't come all the way to meet me, I'll keep walking toward it — one monthly trip at a time — for as long as they'll still get in the car.

**Cutter Rule:** When you can't feel it, show up for it anyway. Presence is a decision, not a feeling — and the people you love keep the showing-up long after they've stopped tracking whether you cried.
