# It Will Go Down

## They kept saying that about the foot. It took about ten years to find out if it was true.

The gauze is the part I'd take back first.

Here's the procedure. They pack the wound with wet gauze. The gauze dries. As it dries it bonds to whatever it's touching, which in my case was an open foot — exposed tissue, exposed bone, whatever was left after the last round of cutting. Then somebody pulls it off, and it takes the dead flesh with it.

That's the whole point. That's what it's for. It's called debridement and it worked and I understand the engineering of it completely.

You know how a Band-Aid sticks to a scab, and there's that half-second where you have to decide whether to do it fast or slow? That, except it's your entire foot, and there is no version where you do it fast enough.

I held the handrails on the bed. Both sides. I would get partway through and pass out — not from choosing to, not from being brave or unbrave about it. The body has a switch for when the input exceeds the budget, and it throws it for you. I've never been grateful for anything the way I was grateful for that switch.

Then they'd finish while I was out, and pack it again, and it would dry again, and we would do it again.

---

They lost count of the surgeries before I did. The number everyone uses is forty, over about a decade, and I want to be careful about that number because it's the thing people latch onto and it's the least interesting fact in this chapter.

Forty is not forty times worse than one. Forty is a different substance entirely. One surgery is an event — it has a before and an after, people bring you things, there's a story arc. Forty surgeries is a *climate.* It's the weather your childhood happens in. You stop asking whether there's another one coming and start asking when.

The first ten were just cutting. Gangrene doesn't negotiate — if you don't take the dead tissue it takes the living tissue, and in 1990 the alternative to taking it was losing the foot. So: ten operations to cut away what had died since the last time they cut.

Somewhere in there they put me on a morphine drip with a button. The button had a design flaw I still think about. You press it, and it doesn't just take the edge off — it puts you under. And you're under long enough that by the time you surface, the same dose doesn't reach anymore. So you learn to ration it. At ten years old I was doing pharmacokinetics on myself with a thumb switch, badly, and losing.

---

Here is what I actually did with that decade, and it's the thing I've never fully explained to anyone.

I turned it into a game. Not in a plucky way. In a systems way.

I ran competitions with myself on the equipment. There was a whole silly economy around the pee bottles and how much and how fast, mostly built to impress the nurses, and the nurses played along, and that was worth real money to me.

And then the actual discipline: I taught myself to bring my own heart rate down. Blood pressure too. There's a monitor right there giving you live feedback on the inside of your own body, and if you're bored enough and horizontal enough for long enough, you start experimenting. Breathe like this, the number goes there. Think about that, the number goes here.

I got good at it. I got good enough that it became a way to pass time that was better than not passing time.

And I started reading my own sensations the way you'd read a gauge. Not to escape them — to *catalogue* them. This ache is different from that ache. This one means the graft. This one means the drain. I built an instrument panel for a body that was mostly bad news, because a bad instrument panel is still better than no information.

I've spent my entire adult career building systems that turn a mess into a dashboard. I did not learn that at work.

---

The big one was the muscle flap.

They took the latissimus dorsi out of my back and moved it onto the top of my foot — blood vessels spliced in, the muscle laid down as new tissue between bone and ligament and whatever skin they could find. Fourteen, fifteen hours. It was 1990 and this was relatively new. My doctors told us that if this had happened five years earlier, the only way to get blood flow to that foot would have been to sew my two feet together and wait.

That surgery is the only one I was ever genuinely afraid I wouldn't wake up from.

We'd planned to take everything from my left side. Partway in, they called an audible and took it from the right instead — the left would have taken too long to harvest and get onto the left foot, and time was the thing they didn't have.

The comment afterward, delivered the way surgeons deliver these things, was that I wouldn't be a professional baseball player or a rock climber, on account of the missing muscle.

I was ten. I had not been planning on either.

---

My mother told me something about that surgery a few weeks ago that I did not know for thirty-six years.

It was the one that scared her most. Not the ten debridements, not the gangrene, not the flight. That one. She said, *I could not even imagine, any way, that you would have your foot.*

And then they brought me out, and the foot was enormous. It didn't look like a foot. It was a swollen mass of transplanted muscle sitting where a foot used to be, and my mother's exact question, standing there looking at it, was: *how is this going to look like a foot again?*

The staff kept telling her the same sentence. *It will go down.*

And my father almost passed out.

I want to sit in that for a second, because I've written a lot of words about Chuck Cutter. He put a live steer on a friend's front lawn in the city of Wooster. He drove a stock car at Michigan Speedway and spun it three hundred and sixty degrees on purpose. He got his legs crushed at sixteen and went back to motorcycles.

He almost went down at the sight of my foot.

My mother didn't. She said it plainly and without any weight on it — *your dad almost passed out, which I said that, and I didn't.* She was the one still standing in that hallway, and she has been the one still standing in every hallway since, and it took me until this year to know it.

---

They tried skin from everywhere. Multiple donor sites, multiple attempts. I asked whether they could take it off my right foot — my good one — because that seemed like obvious matching to me, and they wouldn't do it.

That's when I learned the thing my mother still tells people thirty-six years later: the skin on the bottom of your foot is a material that does not exist anywhere else on your body. Nothing else is built to take that load. You can move skin from a thigh or a back or a calf onto a foot, and it will live there, and it will never be the right stuff.

They also tried to grow it. Several separate times, sourced from further off, using a compound that was close to science fiction in 1991. It worked about as well as anything else did.

That compound is now a spray. If you come into a burn unit today they hit you with it and the skin starts building itself. I use the same class of growth factor on tattoos now, because it cuts the healing time in half.

I don't have anything clever to say about that. Something that was an experimental prescription for a ten-year-old with a destroyed foot is now what you put on a tattoo. That's just how long I've been at this.

---

Somewhere around sophomore year I had a surgery that nobody came to.

CJ had a football game. I understand the logistics — you can't be two places, someone has to be at the game, the surgery was routine by then, and by then "routine" was a word that genuinely applied to me being cut open.

We know what priorities everyone had.

I'm not going to build that into an indictment. My father drove me to every appointment for ten years. My mother worked through all of it so that we could keep the lights on. Nobody in that family was loafing.

But I was in a room by myself getting operated on, and there was a football game, and I noticed, and I have never once forgotten it. Both of those things are allowed to be true.

---

Here's the part I rarely say out loud, and it's the one that still gets me.

Nobody ever told me how to heal.

Stay off it. Keep it elevated. That's it — that's the entire protocol, and it's not complicated, and it is the single highest-leverage piece of information anyone could have handed me at eleven years old.

Nobody handed it to me. Not the surgeons, and — this is the one — not the physical therapists. That was their whole job and it never came up.

So I walked on it. Constantly. And the reason I walked on it is worse than defiance: I couldn't feel it. The nerve damage meant the foot didn't send the signal that would have made a normal kid favor it. So I loaded it, every day, for years, and it healed slower than it needed to for about three decades, and I only worked out the mechanics of my own foot as a grown man.

It still heals, by the way. Thirty-six years on it still heals when I stay off it and keep it up. It would have healed faster back then. That's the sentence I have to live next to.

---

I did not accept any of it until I was a sophomore or a junior in high school.

My mother put that timeline on it and I didn't argue, because it's right. Five, six years of my life spent in a posture of *this is temporary.* Waiting for the version of me that walked into the fifth grade to come back out of the shop.

He wasn't in the shop. There was no shop.

Somewhere around fifteen or sixteen I stopped waiting, and that's the actual end of this chapter — not a surgery, not a discharge, no last stitch. Just a kid quietly closing a ticket he'd had open for six years.

They were right, in the end. It did go down. It took ten years and it never did look like the other one, and it holds me up every day, and I've walked farms and rigs and airports and two daughters down two sets of stairs on a foot made out of my own back.

**Cutter Rule:** Everything they told me was true and none of it was on the timeline I was promised. Take the truth. Throw out the timeline.
