# Five Thousand Dollars

## That's what my accident was worth, and everybody involved thought it was generous.

The woman who hit me sent five thousand dollars from her insurance.

It covered our first deductibles. My mother's word for it, thirty-six years later, was *big.* "That was big to us."

I want you to hold onto the word *first.*

---

What came after the first deductibles was about ten years. Forty-odd surgeries. Ten of those just to cut away tissue that had died since the last cutting. A fourteen-hour operation to move a muscle out of my back and build a foot out of it. Skin harvested from multiple sites, most of it more than once, most of it failing at least once. Grafts that took and grafts that didn't. A morphine drip. Months of inpatient time at Akron Children's, then years of outpatient appointments at a rate I can only describe as a standing obligation.

I did the arithmetic once on the driving alone. Between the appointments and the days after surgeries, I spent something close to **nine months of my life** in the passenger seat of my father's truck.

Not nine months of appointments. Nine months *in the truck.* That's just transit. That's the commute on a ten-year project.

Against all of that: five thousand dollars.

---

Here is the part I found out this month, sitting on the phone with my mother, at forty-six years old.

Nobody ever told them they could pursue a claim.

Her words, and I'm going to quote them exactly because the shape of the sentence is the whole story: *"I think we could have gotten money. I think we probably could have, but nobody said that to us."*

Nobody said that to us.

Not the hospital. Not the second hospital. Not a social worker, not an insurance adjuster, not a lawyer, not one of the forty medical professionals who cut into their child over the following decade and had to know, professionally, what a catastrophic pediatric injury with a motor vehicle involved usually means procedurally.

Not one person, in ten years, said the sentence *you should talk to somebody about this.*

There was a kid from around there — a comparable accident, a couple years off — whose family got a settlement. My mother brought him up herself on that call, unprompted, as the counterexample she'd been carrying around. That family got made whole, or closer to it. Ours got a check for five thousand dollars and a decade of deductibles.

The difference between those two outcomes was not the severity of the injury. It was that somebody, at some point, told one of those families that a question existed.

---

Now, fault. Because I have to be straight about this or the piece is dishonest.

My mother says, twice on that recording, *"we knew it wasn't her fault."*

She's basically right. I came up a farm driveway at full throttle, on a machine twice the size of anything I was supposed to be on, and turned left onto a road I had already decided I wasn't going to fully stop for. She was driving down Rice Hill Road doing nothing wrong at all. She saw me and put that car into a field trying not to hit me and hit me anyway.

If you're asking who caused the collision, it was a ten-year-old, and the ten-year-old was me.

So I'm not writing a piece about a woman who owed my family money. She didn't. She sent five thousand dollars she was in no way obligated to send, to a family she didn't know, after the worst day of her own life, and my mother has been grateful for it for thirty-six years.

The thing I'm writing about is narrower and colder than blame. It's that a family absorbed a decade-long catastrophe without ever being told what the options were — not because anyone lied to them, but because nobody in the room thought it was their job to say it out loud.

Poor information is not the same as bad luck. Bad luck put me in the road. Poor information is what happened for the ten years after.

---

I'd like to know who she was.

My mother can't remember her name. She thinks she was from Creston. That's it — that's the entire surviving record of the other person on that road.

She's a woman who, on an ordinary afternoon in October, watched a child come out of a driveway in front of her, threw her car off the road trying to miss him, and failed. Then sat in that car while he screamed at her from the pavement, because he wouldn't stop screaming long enough for her to go get help.

Then, at some point in the following weeks, she or her insurer sat down and sent five thousand dollars to the family of the kid who ran out in front of her.

I have spent thirty-six years being the boy in that story. I have spent almost none of it thinking about her, and she has probably spent a great deal of hers thinking about me.

If she's alive, she's in her seventies or eighties. I'd like to tell her I walk fine.

---

Here's the ledger nobody wrote down.

My father took me to every single appointment. All of them, for ten years, because his work already had him up in Akron and that's where the hospital was, so the geometry made him the driver. Nine months of truck.

My mother worked through all of it. Not because she wanted to be the one who wasn't there — because someone had to be earning, and the arithmetic of a farm family in 1991 doesn't have a slot for both parents at the hospital.

And her boss paid her anyway.

His name was Jim. She'd used up whatever vacation she had years before the accident finished with us, and Jim kept paying her. Through it. However long it took. My mother's line about him, all these years later, is: *"He paid me no matter what. Well, that was big."*

There's that word again.

That's the actual financial architecture of my childhood, and it isn't insurance and it isn't a settlement. It's a woman from Creston sending money she didn't owe, and a man named Jim quietly deciding not to dock a payroll line, and my father driving nine months' worth of highway.

Three people making up the difference by hand, because the system that was supposed to make up the difference never got mentioned.

---

The scene I can't stop seeing is my mother at the pharmacy counter at Buehler's.

At some point they wanted to try growing skin — a compound, close to experimental in 1991, that was supposed to get the graft sites building tissue instead of just sitting there failing. It was around five hundred dollars. It might have been closer to a thousand.

She went to a grocery store pharmacy in Wayne County, Ohio, and the people behind the counter told her the price, and she said she'd write a check for it.

She told me they *couldn't believe it was that much money.* That's the detail that finishes me — not the price, but that the pharmacy staff were as staggered by it as she was, and she wrote the check standing in front of them anyway.

Her explanation, thirty-six years later, is one sentence: *"but it was something that was good. It was to help grow skin."*

That compound is a spray now. They hit burn patients with it and the skin starts building itself. I use the same class of thing on tattoos, because it halves the healing time, and it costs me nothing I'd notice.

---

I'm not going to tell you this is why I built companies. That's the tidy version and it isn't true — plenty of people grow up broke and don't do what I did, and I had advantages that had nothing to do with a foot.

What it actually did was narrower.

It made me a person who assumes, in every room, that there is a piece of information being withheld — not maliciously, just unspoken, because nobody thinks it's their job to say it. And that the entire difference between the family that gets made whole and the family that gets five thousand dollars is whether somebody says the thing out loud.

So I say the thing out loud. Constantly. To employees, to partners, to my kids. Sometimes past the point where it's welcome.

There's a cost to living like that. It's exhausting and it's not always kind and it has cost me at least one relationship I'd like back.

I'll take it. I watched what the other version costs, and the other version costs ten years, and my mother was grateful the whole time.

**Cutter Rule:** Gratitude and being owed are not opposites. You can be genuinely thankful for the five thousand and still deserve to have been told what the question was.
