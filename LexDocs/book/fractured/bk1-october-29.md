# October 29

## My father taught us to put the hitch bolt in upside down.

The idea was that you wouldn't lose the nut. Bolt goes up through the hole instead of down through it, nut sits on top, gravity holds it in place while you're rattling across a field. It was a real solution to a real problem and it worked most of the time.

What it did not solve was that a nut on top of a bolt on a machine that vibrates for four miles will eventually walk itself off anyway. Then the bolt drops out of the hole, and the wagon you were pulling is no longer attached to anything.

We knew that. When two of us rode together, one of us watched the bolt. That was the whole system — a kid facing backwards, checking a nut every couple of minutes.

That day there was only one of us.

---

I was ten years old. There was a pizza party the next day for winning the soccer championship, and I was the goalie.

I want to be honest about how I ended up a goalie, because it explains more about me than the accident does. I was a football kid. I made fun of the kids who played soccer. Then I got in trouble at lunch and got put on the green bench — the bench they'd placed right outside the cafeteria door so that every single kid walking out to recess had to see you sitting on it. The art teacher, who also coached soccer, walked past and asked if I'd like to get off the bench.

I said yes. I picked goalie because you got a better shirt and gloves and you didn't have to run.

I was the fastest kid on that team. I just didn't want to run.

---

We got off the bus in the afternoon. The four-wheelers were how we got anywhere, and running them meant hauling gas cans down to the Boreman farm a few miles off — the first wells my dad ever drilled, back in the early eighties, which is how he ended up friends with the father and the triplet sons, Rick, Randy, and Rod. Rod's boy Tony was my best friend. That's how everything worked out there. The gas came from a friendship that came from a well.

Normally CJ drove and I rode on the back and watched the bolt.

That day CJ made me an offer. He'd stay home. I'd go alone. And I could take the 125 Honda — the big one, the one with the rack on the back.

I rode a 60cc three-wheeler. My own machine was a yellow Yamaha. CJ's runabout was an 80cc blue four-wheeler. The 125 was roughly double anything I'd ever been allowed to sit on, and my brother was handing it to me like it was nothing.

My brother was a salesman. He always knew exactly what to say to get a person to do a thing. He didn't lie to me. He just didn't mention the part where our mother was coming home to take us into town, and that town meant McDonald's, and that McDonald's was a genuinely enormous event in our lives.

He helped me hook up the wagon. He put the bolt in.

I heard "you can take the big four-wheeler" and I was already gone.

---

The Boreman place sat at the bottom of a long driveway that ran down the hill on the one side of the farm. I was two-thirds of the way there, pulling an empty red wagon, when the bolt did what the bolt does. The wagon came off the hitch and dug itself into the ground behind me.

Twenty minutes. Down to the farm, find a pin, back up the hill, get it hooked, get moving again. Alone, on my knees in the dirt, working the hitch with my hands, thinking about McDonald's.

Then I got down to the tank and they told me they were out of gas. Come back another day.

That's when it landed — the whole shape of it, all at once, standing next to an empty tank with two empty cans and a wagon I'd just spent twenty minutes reattaching. *That's* why he wanted to stay home. Mom was coming. He'd be there and I wouldn't.

So I made a decision, and I want to be precise about it, because I've spent thirty-six years being precise about it: I left the wagon at the Boreman farm. I left the empty cans with it. Without the wagon I could go twice as fast, and going twice as fast was the only way I was getting home in time.

I dropped the load and I opened it up.

---

I don't remember the ride. I remember the idea of the ride — the dust coming up off that driveway in a wall behind me, the machine finally being asked to do the thing it was built for, my whole body committed to a schedule.

At the top of the driveway was Rice Hill Road. You slowed for it. You almost never stopped, because there was almost never anything on it.

I eased off the throttle and turned left.

Something said *look.*

I've never been able to tell you what that was. Not a thought, exactly. Not a word out loud. Something in me raised its hand about a half-second too late to matter.

Then time did the thing that everybody describes and nobody believes until it happens to them. It got wide. I had all the time in the world and no ability to use any of it, and what I did with it was look through a windshield at a woman's face.

I've thought about her face more than almost anything else from that day. Because it was pure terror, and none of it was for her. She was about to run over a ten-year-old and she knew it, and I watched her understand it, and I watched her yank that car as hard left as it would go — off the road, down into the field — and it still wasn't enough.

The car took the four-wheeler apart. It took my left foot with it. My foot was pinned between the foot peg and the bumper, and my foot lost.

I went about twenty feet.

---

I woke up in the middle of the road. My helmet was shattered — that's where the stitches in my forehead came from. She was still in the car.

I remember her getting out and walking up onto the road toward me and I remember screaming at her: *go get someone.*

Neighbors came. They'd heard it.

I pulled my sock off.

I don't know why. I think I needed to see. And what I saw was the skin flip over my toes — the whole thing degloved, the way you'd peel a wet glove off a hand — and I understood in that second that this was not stitches. I'd had stitches. I had eighty-seven of them before this day, and half of those were CJ's doing. Stitches were a thing that happened to me on a schedule. This was a different category of event.

So I held my own leg up by the calf to keep the foot off the road. And with every heartbeat a new squirt of blood landed on the asphalt.

I'm not going to make that into anything. That's just what it did. Heartbeat, blood on the road. Heartbeat, blood on the road.

Mike Krueger's dad got there within minutes — he worked at the paint factory nearby, and I knew his face, which mattered more than I can explain. Joe Rice was the first EMT on the scene. He'd graduated high school with my mother.

That's the part people from cities don't understand about that place. The man who put me in the squad went to school with my mom.

I was crying and I could not stop saying the same two things. *I'm not going to be able to walk. I'm not going to be able to wrestle.*

At ten years old, in the road, with my foot in my hand, those were the two things I could think to grieve.

---

By the time my mother got there they had me loaded, and they would not let her see me and they would not let her ride.

She followed the squad to Wooster in her own car. She has never described that drive to me. I have never asked.

I don't remember her at the hospital at all. What I remember is my father coming through the emergency room door and standing over the bed.

Here's the part I rarely say out loud: I had never seen that face on him. Chuck Cutter had a face for everything, and most of them were some flavor of *I've got this.* This wasn't that one. I know now what I was looking at, because I've been a father to a hurt kid since. It's helplessness. It's the specific look of a man who fixes things standing next to a thing he cannot fix.

And he was the one person in that room who knew exactly what I was in for, because he'd been sixteen years old in a bed like that one with his legs crushed. Neither of us said a word about it. Not that day. Not for years.

Then the doctors told me two things: I was going to Akron Children's by helicopter, and they were going to put a catheter in.

I had no say in the first one. So I made my entire stand on the second.

Every time they came at me with it, I peed. Every time. I had one variable left in the whole world that I controlled and I spent it emptying my bladder at the medical staff of Wooster Community Hospital. They gave up eventually — obviously they just handled it once I was under, which I understood even at the time and didn't care about even a little.

I got two and a half hours there. Then they wheeled me out to the helicopter.

I remember the wheeling. I don't remember the flight.

---

I've run the debrief on that afternoon more times than on any system I've ever built, and the chain is clean:

The bolt drops. Twenty minutes gone. There's no gas, so I don't spend thirty minutes filling cans. No cans means no weight, so I don't have to crawl home. No wagon means I can go twice as fast, so I do.

Take away any single one of those and I'm not on Rice Hill Road at 4:15. Two seconds earlier and it's a close call I'd have told at dinner. Two seconds later and she's past the driveway and I never knew she existed.

The chain is complete. Every link is a small mechanical fact with a cause I can name. It's the cleanest root-cause analysis I'll ever do, and I did the first draft of it at ten years old in a hospital bed, because working the problem was the only thing left that I was any good at.

And it explains everything about that day except the one thing I actually want explained.

Something said *look.*

I've been an engineer my whole adult life. I don't have a slot for that. It wasn't early enough to save me and it wasn't nothing. Thirty-six years on it's still sitting there in the middle of an otherwise perfect fault tree, and I don't get to remove it, and I can't explain it.

**Cutter Rule:** You can trace every cause and still not be handed a reason. Do the debrief anyway. Then live with the part that won't reduce.
