# Speed Is All That Matters

Speed is all that matters.

Not features. Not headcount. Not a patent on the wall.

Speed.

I'm a 6th gen farmer and a 2nd gen energy operator from West Salem, Ohio. Oil and gas wells have been on our family farm my entire life.

Farming and energy move slowly for good reasons. Soil and steel don't care about your roadmap.

But the software that runs those industries is about to change faster than anything I've seen.

The winners won't be the ones with the biggest engineering teams. They'll be the ones who can see a problem on Monday and have it solved in the field by Friday.

This is my manifesto on how to build in that world.

## The bottleneck moved

For decades, engineering was the bottleneck.

You hired engineers. You wrote specs. You waited for sprints. You hoped what came back resembled what you asked for.

The distance between the person who understood the problem and the person who could build the solution was measured in meetings.

AI is taking engineering and software out of the bottleneck.

Engineering judgment still matters. But turning an idea into working software has collapsed from months to days. Sometimes hours.

When building gets cheap, the ability to build is no longer the scarce thing. The scarce thing is knowing exactly what to build, and moving before anyone else does.

That changes who you need in the room.

## Replace the engineer with the engineering operator

This isn't about getting rid of engineers. The role changes.

The engineer of the last era translated requirements into code. The engineering operator of this era runs the whole loop: understands the business, directs AI to build, tests it against reality, and ships.

They own outcomes, not tickets.

An engineering operator doesn't ask, "What's the spec?" They ask, "What's broken in the field today, and how fast can we fix it?"

One engineering operator with AI can now do what used to take a team, because there are no handoffs. No translation layer. No telephone game.

The person who sees the problem builds the fix.

## Forget forward deployed. Fully immersed.

The tech world loves the forward deployed engineer. Embed a smart technologist with the customer, learn the business, bring the lessons home.

That's not enough anymore. Forward deployed still means visiting. You're a guest in somebody else's industry.

Forget forward deployed operators. I'm talking about fully immersed operators.

People who live inside the industry. People who know what a planting window feels like when the weather won't cooperate. People who don't need the problem explained to them, because it's their problem too.

I didn't learn the oilfield in a discovery call. I grew up with wells on the farm.

Put AI in the hands of a fully immersed operator and something different happens. They don't build what a customer described. They build what they already know the industry needs, because they've lived it.

The gap between the domain and the software disappears.

That's where real speed comes from. Not from coding faster. From never having to be told.

## Where I learned this

I'm not theorizing.

I founded Fielding, an oilfield software company. I coded Fielding at my desk, rocking my daughter to sleep. Its systems went on to monitor hundreds of thousands of wells across North America, and Fielding was acquired by Quorum.

The lightbulb came in West Texas.

We monitored a 40,000-well CO2 enhanced oil recovery field: injection wells, producing wells, oil cuts, CO2 injections. The company paid for CO2 captured from a nearby power plant and put it to work underground.

That's where it clicked. The software wasn't just counting barrels. It held the whole system in one place: the field, the economics, and the captured carbon.

And it proved something I still believe: "We do the right thing when we can make money on it."

Fielding didn't work because we had the most engineers or the cleverest IP. It worked because we understood the oilfield from the inside, and we moved faster than people who didn't.

## Meetings are a speed tax

After the acquisition, I learned what slow looks like up close.

I hated oversized meetings. I'd look around the room, estimate everyone's salary tier, and do the math: $3,000 to $4,000 an hour.

If a meeting had no value, I walked out. I wanted a cost ticker in Outlook, so everyone could watch the meter run.

That's not a knock on any one company. It happens wherever headcount scales faster than speed.

Every extra person in the room is a handoff. Every handoff is delay. And delay is the most expensive line item nobody budgets for.

## Do patents even matter anymore?

Here's a question I keep asking myself as a founder: how much do patents even matter in a world where we can build anything?

Software patents especially. I don't see the point of keeping a lot of them. They're easily replaceable.

If someone can describe what your patent does to an AI and have a working alternative by the end of the week, what exactly are you protecting?

A patent is a wall. Walls made sense when building was slow and copying was expensive.

Today a wall mostly marks the spot where you stopped moving.

The real moat is being able to adapt, grow and scale quickly. It's the speed of your learning loop: how fast you hear from the field, change the product, and get it back into someone's hands.

Nobody can patent that. Nobody can copy it either. By the time they copy what you shipped, you've already shipped the next thing.

I'd rather spend my energy running than guarding a fence.

## Death of the OS. Long live the industry OS.

Don't believe it? Look at Microsoft.

Microsoft built one of the greatest businesses in history on the operating system. Then it made the operating system a free upgrade, and the money moved up the stack: into the cloud, into productivity, into the services that sit on top.

The operating system didn't disappear. It just stopped being where the value lives.

That's the lesson. A generic operating system is table stakes now.

The value is in the operating system that bakes the domain in. One that gives the user something bespoke, but carries all the intelligence of the industry. One that already knows how a well produces, how a crop gets planted, how power gets from the ground to the grid.

Generic software asks the user to adapt to it. An industry operating system adapts to the user and brings the whole industry's knowledge along.

AI is what makes that possible: software that is deeply specialized and personal at the same time.

That's not a feature. That's a new category.

Death of the operating system. Long live the industry operating system.

## Slow down to speed up

Speed is not recklessness. I learned that the hard way.

In 2019 and 2020, I picked the wrong teams. By 2022 I knew I was the problem, and I had to slow down to speed up.

I cut the relationships that sucked the energy out of everything. I got honest about who I needed beside me.

Speed doesn't come from headcount. It comes from the right small team, or from the operator doing the work himself.

A few people who trust each other and know the domain will outrun a hundred who need a meeting to agree.

## Work is fun again

After Quorum, my view was more of a venture studio. The IP has to stay with the company, not with Shawn Cutter. I didn't want to be acquired again, or be a necessary component.

I wanted work to be fun and part of life.

Fast forward to right now. AI has enabled that.

I also know myself. I get bored once I figure something out. I need a problem so big it's never solved, one that always evolves.

I picked energy, food, and AI.

I am building operating systems. EnergiAcres, Terrasow and Fielding are examples. Each one stands on its own, in its own industry.

- Fielding OS is for oil and gas.
- Terrasow, which I started in 2020 and where I learned to build with a team, is an AI-first operating system for farmers.
- EnergiAcres is energy plus acres, with AI in the middle.

Separate businesses. Same thesis: bake the domain in, put fully immersed operators at the controls, and move fast.

When the distance between an idea and a working product is an afternoon, you stop dreading the build. You try more things. You learn faster. You fail cheaper.

Speed isn't just a competitive advantage. It's a better way to live.

## What I believe

- Engineering is no longer the bottleneck. Knowing what to build is.
- Replace the engineer with the engineering operator.
- Forget forward deployed. Fully immersed operators win.
- Meetings are a speed tax. Stop paying it.
- In a world where we can build anything, patents matter less. Your speed matters more.
- The generic operating system is commoditized. The industry operating system is where the value is.
- Speed isn't recklessness. Slow down to speed up, then pick the right small team.

If you're inside an industry with real dirt under its fingernails (farming, energy, construction, logistics, anything), this is your moment.

You don't have to wait for a software company to understand you. You can build it yourself, faster than they ever could.

Stop guarding. Start shipping.

Speed is all that matters.

— Shawn Cutter
6th gen farmer, 2nd gen energy operator
